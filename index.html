1. Why inventory planning matters

Inventory planning decides what to stock, how much to stock, and when to replenish it. The goal is to meet customer demand without tying up too much money in excess inventory.

Too little stock

Can cause stockouts, delayed orders, lost sales, and poor service levels.

Too much stock

Increases working capital, storage costs, obsolescence, and risk of damage.

Good planning

Balances availability, cost, lead time, and uncertainty using clear replenishment rules.

2. Key inventory terms

The reorder point tells you when to order. Reorder quantity tells you how much to order.

Buffer

Safety stock

Extra inventory held to protect against unexpected demand or supply delays.

Trigger

Reorder point (ROP)

The inventory position at which a replenishment order should be triggered.

Order size

Reorder quantity

The quantity to purchase or replenish when an order is triggered.

Upper target

Maximum stock

The desired upper inventory level after replenishment, based on the chosen policy.

Lower threshold

Minimum stock

Often used operationally to mean the reorder threshold. The exact definition can vary by company or system.

How the levels fit together

Conceptual illustration, not a scale drawing. Safety stock is included in the reorder-point calculation; it is not a separate order trigger.

Core formulas

Reorder point = Expected demand during lead time + Safety stock
Inventory position = On-hand stock + On-order stock − Backorders
Order quantity (order-up-to policy) = Maximum stock − Inventory position

In a fixed-order-quantity policy, the order quantity may instead be a predefined lot size whenever the reorder point is reached.

Minimum stock: in many min–max systems, minimum stock is configured as the reorder point. Some systems use “minimum” differently, so confirm the business definition before comparing reports.

3. A practical example

Suppose a service depot uses an average of 5 replacement units per day. Supplier lead time is 8 days, and the planner chooses 15 units of safety stock.

InputValueMeaning
Average daily demand5 unitsExpected daily consumption
Lead time8 daysTime between ordering and receipt
Safety stock15 unitsExtra buffer for uncertainty
Maximum stock70 unitsIllustrative order-up-to target
Lead-time demand = 5 × 8 = 40 units
Reorder point = 40 + 15 = 55 units

When inventory position reaches 55 units, the replenishment trigger is reached. If inventory position is 55 and the maximum target is 70, an order-up-to policy suggests an order of 15 units.

Assumption: demand is steady in this simple example. Real planning should consider demand variability, lead-time variability, review frequency, order constraints, and service-level targets.

4. Four types of demand

Some products are used regularly; others have long periods with no demand. Classifying demand helps planners choose suitable forecasting and replenishment methods.

Smooth

Regular demand

Demand occurs frequently and quantities are relatively stable.

Example: 8, 9, 8, 10, 9, 8 units each month.

Typical approach: moving averages or conventional time-series forecasting.

Erratic

Variable quantities

Demand occurs frequently, but the quantity changes significantly.

Example: 3, 18, 5, 22, 4, 15 units each month.

Typical approach: model variability and compare robust forecasting baselines.

Intermittent

Many zero periods

Demand occurs infrequently, but non-zero quantities are relatively consistent.

Example: 0, 0, 4, 0, 0, 5, 0, 0 units.

Typical approach: Croston-style methods or event/usage-based planning.

Lumpy

Sparse and variable

Demand is infrequent and quantities vary substantially when demand occurs.

Example: 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 17, 0, 6 units.

Typical approach: segmentation, scenarios, and criticality-based policies.

Visual intuition

Illustrative patterns only; not actual product data.

How demand is classified: ADI and CV²

  • ADI (Average Demand Interval): average number of periods between non-zero demand occurrences.
  • CV² (Squared coefficient of variation): squared variation in the size of non-zero demand quantities.
ADI = Number of periods ÷ Number of non-zero demand periods
CV² = (Standard deviation of non-zero demand ÷ Mean non-zero demand)²
Demand typeADICV²Interpretation
Smooth≤ 1.32≤ 0.49Frequent, stable quantities
Erratic≤ 1.32> 0.49Frequent, variable quantities

Lumpy> 1.32> 0.49Infrequent, variable quantities

These are commonly used Syntetos–Boylan classification thresholds. They are practical guidelines, not universal laws; results depend on the time bucket, history window, and data treatment.

6. Useful inventory KPIs

Service level

How often demand is fulfilled from available stock, using the organisation's chosen definition.

Stockout rate

Frequency or proportion of demand occasions where stock was unavailable.

Inventory turns

How many times inventory is consumed or sold over a period; often annualised COGS divided by average inventory value.

Excess & obsolete

Value or quantity of inventory beyond expected need or no longer usable.

Forecast error

Difference between forecast and actual demand; choose metrics suited to intermittent demand.

Supplier lead time

Time from placing an order to receiving usable inventory.

Final takeaway

Inventory planning is not simply about keeping more stock. It is about setting sensible replenishment triggers and quantities while balancing customer service, supply risk, and working capital. Understand the demand pattern, then choose a policy that fits the product and business context.
